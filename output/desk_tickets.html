Expected

Run reflection

Reflection

This run showed me a team that was careful with money and shop facts, but not always economical with its own conversations. The Ticket tabs preserve the plan-versus-run evidence; the Cash tab is the arithmetic check, not just a dashboard decoration.

How the agents did, ticket by ticket

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee

Performance: The investigation got the important facts right: size S had zero stock, invoice 501 for $840 was overdue, and Bulldog Print Co's listed five-day lead time was not a guaranteed arrival date. Accounting checked cash and correctly asked for human approval; no agent paid or called the vendor. After the user approved both the invoice and the separate $8 one-tee purchase order, the order-placement task was closed with delivery explicitly pending. That distinction matters: the database still has zero tees, so the page does not pretend the product was received.

Expected vs. Actual: I expected Boss → Inventory, then Inventory → Accounting, then Boss → Customer Service for a careful customer draft. Boss did send work to Inventory and Accounting, but the actual peer loop went Boss → Inventory, Boss → Accounting, Inventory → Accounting, then Accounting → Inventory. That last return trip was repetitive. Customer Service was never asked, so the expected draft was missing. The actual MCP calls did cover list_open_tickets, customer-order context, vendor restock context, and accounting context, though some context reads were duplicated. The initial attempt also failed before tool work because of a Portkey reasoning-effort/tool compatibility setting; the failed attempt stayed in the audit trail, and the retry used the same required model.

Ticket 102 — rent notice

Performance: This was the cleanest run. Facilities used the shop's date, 2026-08-31, and correctly found the $2,400 rent was due on 2026-09-02—not overdue. Accounting checked the balance and left the payment for a human. After approval, the rent payment was recorded, the lease next-due date advanced to 2026-10-02, and the ticket resolved. This respected the date rule and the payment guardrail.

Expected vs. Actual: My plan was Boss → Facilities → Accounting. Actual was Boss → Facilities and Boss → Accounting in parallel; Facilities did not delegate. Both routes were sensible, and the required rent and accounting tools were used. The difference is mostly orchestration: Boss got the cash check directly instead of having Facilities request it. There was a repeated rent-context read, but no circular delegation or missing specialist.

Ticket 103 — bulk hoodie discount

Performance: Inventory established 8 listed medium hoodies against 20 requested; Accounting correctly treated $22 cost and $58 list price as evidence, not permission to promise a discount; Customer Service produced a cautious, unsent draft. The $264 cost of the 12-unit shortfall exceeded the checking balance, so no purchase or discount was made. The human resolution closed the request without granting a discount. This was safe, but the investigation was incomplete: the planned vendor restock and full cash/payables checks never happened through MCP.

Expected vs. Actual: I expected Inventory to check vendor lead time and invoice eligibility, delegate a margin/cash check to Accounting, and then have Customer Service draft only after the facts were settled. Actual was Boss → Inventory, Boss → Accounting, and Boss → Customer Service, with all four agents reading get_bulk_discount_context. No one called get_vendor_restock_context or get_accounting_context, so the plan's supplier and cash checks were missed. The later database-backed human resolution still refused the unaffordable restock; the Customer Service draft remained unsent, as required.

What would be simpler with one agent and tools?

Ticket 102 is the clearest one-agent case: one well-instructed operator could read get_rent_ticket_context, compare desk.date_today with the lease date, read get_accounting_context, and return one recommendation plus a human-approval request. The decision is a short chain of facts, and a second agent added little beyond a useful check.

Even Ticket 101 could have been one Boss run with a required checklist—customer-order context, vendor-restock context, accounting context—then a Customer Service draft as a structured output field. That would have avoided the Inventory ↔ Accounting loop and duplicate reads. Ticket 103 could likewise be one sequential analysis of stock, price, vendor eligibility, cash, and a draft. The five-agent setup helps divide expertise and audit who contributed, but the trace shows that unrestricted peer delegation can burn calls without adding evidence. A more efficient version would keep the specialists, but have Boss delegate only distinct questions and check that each required tool was actually used before deciding.

Three new problems this team could handle with its current tools

  1. A second apparel customer order has a size shortfall and its printer has an unpaid bill. Boss can route to Inventory for get_customer_order_context and get_vendor_restock_context, then to Accounting for get_accounting_context. As in Ticket 101, it can identify a shipment hold, calculate whether a payment is affordable, and ask a human—without contacting the printer or promising a date.
  2. Next month's rent notice arrives for a lease already on the books. Facilities can use get_rent_ticket_context and the shop date, Accounting can check cash/payables, and the approval route can record rent and advance the lease due date. Ticket 102 is the working example of this flow.
  3. A different product receives a bulk-price request. For a matching price_override ticket, Inventory and Accounting can use get_bulk_discount_context to compare requested quantity, stock, unit cost, and list price; Customer Service can draft a cautious reply. Ticket 103 shows the boundary: the team can assess and draft, but it must not invent stock or issue an unsupported discount.

Three new problems this team could not solve with its current tools

  1. Actually email a customer about a delay, accept a cancellation, or confirm fulfillment. Customer Service only returns a draft; there is no contact-preference lookup, outbound-mail tool, delivery confirmation, or customer-reply inbox. Add a consent-aware customer communications tool and a human-reviewed send/reply workflow. Customer Service could own the wording, with Boss supervising the approval boundary. In Ticket 101, the board deliberately kept the message unsent.
  2. Track a shipment and receive goods into inventory. The approval route records a purchase-order payment, but there is no purchase-order/shipment table, vendor confirmation, delivery scan, or inventory-receipt tool. Ticket 101 ends with a paid $8 order and a five-day listed lead time while stock remains zero—payment is not proof of delivery. Add purchase-order, shipment-status, and receive-inventory MCP tools (with human approval and an audit trail), plus a Purchasing/Logistics role working with Inventory. The current assignment still forbids real vendor contact, so these would need a simulated or explicitly approved integration.
  3. Schedule a repair at the leased shop, such as a leak or failed refrigerator. Facilities can inspect leases and rent dates, but the database has no maintenance requests, equipment, work orders, repair vendors, or service appointments. Add maintenance/work-order tables and tools to log severity, assign an approved technician, and track completion; Facilities could triage, with a Maintenance Coordinator agent for repair scope and safety. Ticket 102 proves the existing facilities scope is rent/lease administration, not physical repairs.
